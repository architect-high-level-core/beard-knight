## What causes body odor in men?

Cleveland Clinic puts it simply: body odor is caused by a mix of bacteria and sweat on your skin. Our skin is naturally covered with bacteria. When we sweat, the water, salt and fat in sweat mix with these bacteria and can produce an odor that may be unpleasant, pleasant or barely noticeable. The medical term for body odor is bromhidrosis.

Not all sweat glands are equal. The eccrine glands, found over most of the body, produce the watery sweat that cools you down. The apocrine glands, concentrated in the armpits and groin, are the ones most associated with odor. That is why underarm odor is the classic problem, and why a fresh shirt alone rarely fixes it.

Several factors make body odor stronger, according to Cleveland Clinic:

- **Exercise, hot weather and stress or anxiety.** Stress can activate the apocrine glands, which is why a tense meeting can leave a sharper smell than a gym session.
- **Diet.** Sulfur-rich foods such as onions, garlic, cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower and red meat may affect how you smell. Caffeine, spicy food, hot sauce and alcohol can make you sweat more.
- **Weight and genetics.** Having overweight or obesity and your genes both play a role.
- **Hormones and medications.** Both can change your natural body odor.

Mayo Clinic adds body hair, fever, heat exposure and drinking too much alcohol to the list of risk factors, and notes that body odor can smell tangy, oniony, sour or otherwise strong. A persistent change in your body odor despite showering and changing clothes is worth noting: Cleveland Clinic says it can occasionally point to an underlying condition such as diabetes, liver disease or kidney disease.

## Deodorant vs antiperspirant: what is the difference?

This is the most important distinction in men's underarm care, and it is often misunderstood. Cleveland Clinic explains that an antiperspirant reduces the amount of sweat your body makes. Its main active ingredients are aluminum salts, which restrict access to the upper part of the sweat gland, so it is harder for sweat to reach the skin. Less sweat means fewer bacteria feeding on it, which means less odor.

A deodorant does not block sweat. It aims to neutralize or cover the smell, using ingredients that target odor-causing bacteria plus fragrance, perfume or essential oils. In the United States, antiperspirants are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), while deodorants are not.

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) lists the typical deodorant ingredients:

- **Fragrance** to cover body odor.
- **Absorbers** that soak up moisture and feel smooth, such as cornstarch, arrowroot powder, tapioca or silica.
- **Odor fighters** that target the bacteria behind body odor, including witch hazel, mandelic acid, baking soda or tea tree oil.

The AAD's rule of thumb is useful when reading a label: unless you see an aluminum ingredient such as aluminum chloride, aluminum chlorohydrate or aluminum zirconium, the product is not an antiperspirant and will not reduce how much you sweat.

So which should you choose? If your main concern is smell, a good deodorant may be enough. If you sweat through shirts, an antiperspirant is the tool designed for the job. Many men use both: an antiperspirant at night and a fragranced deodorant in the morning.

## How to apply antiperspirant: at night, on dry skin

Most men apply antiperspirant after the morning shower. Dermatologists suggest the opposite timing. A Cleveland Clinic expert explains that many antiperspirants work best when applied at night, when sweating is at a minimum. That lets the product stay in place for six to eight hours before you wash in the morning, and you can then use a deodorant to handle odor during the day.

The International Hyperhidrosis Society describes the mechanism: once applied, sweat in the underarm dissolves the antiperspirant particles and pulls them into the pores, forming superficial plugs just below the skin's surface. When the body senses that the sweat duct is plugged, a feedback mechanism stops the flow. These plugs can stay in place for at least 24 hours before they are gradually washed away, which is why a nighttime application still protects you the next day, even after a shower.

A simple routine based on AAD and International Hyperhidrosis Society guidance:

1. **Make sure the skin is completely dry.** Applying to dry skin reduces the chance of irritation. Pat the armpits dry with a towel first.
2. **Apply at bedtime and let it dry completely** before going to bed.
3. **Use a deodorant in the morning** if you want fragrance or extra odor protection.
4. **Be careful after shaving.** Cleveland Clinic warns that putting antiperspirant or deodorant on freshly shaved, irritated skin can cause a rash or irritant contact dermatitis.
5. **Step up gradually.** The International Hyperhidrosis Society suggests starting with gentle over-the-counter formulas, then moving to clinical-strength products and, if needed, prescription formulas.

If an antiperspirant is part of a treatment plan for excessive sweating, the AAD says you continue the bedtime routine until sweating is under control, which usually happens within a few weeks, and then apply as needed, usually once or twice a week.

Two expert points matter before you switch:

- **Expect an adjustment period.** A dermatologist quoted by Robb Report warns that stopping an aluminum product can bring more sweat and an unfamiliar body odor at first, and that this interim phase can last from a few days to three to four weeks. Robb Report also suggests winter as the best time to switch, because cold, dry weather means less sweat and a longer runway to adapt.

Keep expectations realistic: an aluminum-free deodorant is an odor product. It can feel drier thanks to starches and absorbers, but it will not stop sweat the way an antiperspirant does.

## Is aluminum in antiperspirant safe?

Much of the aluminum-free trend is driven by worry rather than evidence, so it helps to know what cancer experts actually say. The American Cancer Society (ACS) states that research does not show a link between aluminum compounds in antiperspirants and breast cancer. Most studies on the topic are case-control studies with mixed results, and a larger, well-designed study found no link between breast cancer and antiperspirant use, deodorant use or underarm shaving.

On absorption, the ACS cites a study that found only a tiny fraction of aluminum from an underarm antiperspirant, about 0.012%, was absorbed, far less than what a person absorbs from food over the same time. None of the major health organizations the ACS relies on, including the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the National Toxicology Program and the FDA, has found conclusive evidence linking antiperspirants or aluminum to breast cancer risk.

What about the popular idea of an "armpit detox"? The ACS is clear: sweat glands mainly cool the body rather than remove toxins, and most cancer-causing substances are removed by the kidneys and the liver. There is no need to detox before switching products.

The practical conclusion: choose aluminum-free if you prefer the feel, the scent or the philosophy, or if your doctor advises it, but there is no evidence-based reason to fear a well-made antiperspirant if you need sweat control.

## Underarm care, body wash and the whole-body deodorant question

The best deodorant works better on clean skin. Cleveland Clinic recommends a daily bath or shower with an antibacterial soap, focusing on the areas where you sweat the most, such as the armpits and groin, because removing some of the bacteria regularly helps prevent odor. It also suggests keeping underarm hair short or shaved so sweat evaporates more quickly, washing clothes regularly and choosing loose-fitting cotton or other moisture-wicking fabrics. In its guide to foods and body odor, Cleveland Clinic adds that synthetic fabrics such as polyester and nylon can trap sweat and bacteria and magnify the smell, and that towels and bathrobes should be washed regularly.

Whole-body deodorants are the newest addition to many shower routines. The AAD's verdict is nuanced:

- **Apply only where odor starts.** Dermatologists say body odor comes mainly from the armpits, feet and groin. Using the product elsewhere will not reduce the smell and may irritate the skin.
- **No fragrance on the groin.** The AAD warns that fragranced products in the groin area increase the risk of irritating sensitive skin and may cause a yeast infection.
- **It is still a deodorant.** Most whole-body deodorants do not reduce sweating, because they are not antiperspirants.

For a premium underarm routine, keep it simple: wash with a gentle antibacterial or odor-fighting body wash, pat completely dry, apply your antiperspirant at night or your deodorant in the morning, and choose a breathable cotton or linen shirt. For feet, the AAD advises changing footwear often, wearing socks that wick moisture and not wearing the same shoes two days in a row so they can dry out.

## Deodorant and cologne pairing: how to layer scents

A gentleman's scent wardrobe should not compete with itself. A strongly perfumed sport deodorant under a refined vetiver eau de parfum creates a muddled trail. That is exactly why fragrance-led deodorants are booming: Valet notes that Le Labo's roll-on carries the brand's signature grooming fragrance, and that the scents of Salt & Stone's gel, such as santal and vetiver or bergamot and hinoki, rival niche colognes in complexity. Nécessaire, it adds, excels at designer-level fragrances, with notes such as hinoki, basil, magnolia and vetiver in one stick.

Practical pairing rules:

- **Match the family.** A woody deodorant (cedar, sandalwood, vetiver, hinoki) sits naturally under a woody or aromatic cologne. A citrus or green deodorant suits fresh colognes built on bergamot, neroli or lemon.
- **Go unscented under complex fragrances.** If you wear a rich oud, leather or amber scent, a fragrance-free deodorant lets the cologne speak alone.
- **Use the same line if it exists.** Many fragrance houses sell matching deodorant sticks. They are designed to blend with the cologne rather than fight it.
- **Never use cologne as a deodorant.** Fragrance only masks odor. It does not remove sweat or bacteria, so a quick spritz after the gym is no substitute for a shower.

GQ adds an interesting point about subtle, natural-smelling formulas: its tester found that Ursa Major's forest scent mingled with his skin rather than masking it, which is the effect most men want from a deodorant worn with a fine fragrance.

## When sweating is a medical issue: hyperhidrosis

Some men sweat far more than heat, exercise or stress can explain. Mayo Clinic defines hyperhidrosis as excessive sweating that is not always related to heat or exercise. You may sweat so much that it soaks through your clothes or drips off your hands. The type that usually affects the hands, feet, underarms or face causes at least one episode a week while you are awake, and the sweating usually happens on both sides of the body. Primary hyperhidrosis is linked to faulty nerve signals that make the eccrine sweat glands overactive, and it can run in families.

Mayo Clinic advises seeing a health care provider if:

- Sweating disrupts your daily routine.
- Sweating causes emotional distress or social withdrawal.
- You suddenly begin to sweat more than usual.
- You experience night sweats for no apparent reason.

Mayo Clinic's page on sweating and body odor adds an unexplained change in your body odor and a family history of hyperhidrosis to that list. Seek immediate medical attention if heavy sweating comes with dizziness, chest pain, nausea, or cold skin and a rapid pulse.

Treatment usually helps. The AAD notes that non-prescription antiperspirants are only effective for mild hyperhidrosis, and many patients need a prescription antiperspirant. Its self-care tips include keeping a sweat journal to identify triggers such as heat, spicy foods, caffeine, alcohol and anxiety, wearing breathable fabrics, carrying a spare shirt and drinking plenty of water. Mayo Clinic adds that when antiperspirants are not enough, other medications and therapies are available, and in severe cases surgery may be considered.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between deodorant and antiperspirant?

A deodorant fights or masks body odor but does not reduce sweating. An antiperspirant contains aluminum salts that reduce how much you sweat, which can also reduce odor. If you mainly want to smell fresh, a deodorant may be enough; if you sweat through shirts, use an antiperspirant.

### When is the best time to apply antiperspirant?

At night before bed, on completely dry skin. Sweating is at its lowest at night, so the active ingredient has time to form plugs in the sweat ducts, and the effect lasts into the next day even after a shower. Use a deodorant in the morning if you want fragrance.

### Does aluminum-free deodorant stop sweat?

No. Aluminum-free deodorants control odor and may feel drier thanks to absorbers such as tapioca starch or arrowroot, but only aluminum-based antiperspirants reduce sweating. Expect an adjustment period of a few days to about three to four weeks when you switch.

### Is aluminum in antiperspirant dangerous?

According to the American Cancer Society, research does not show a link between aluminum antiperspirants and breast cancer, and only a tiny fraction of aluminum, about 0.012% in one study, is absorbed through the skin. Major health agencies have found no conclusive evidence of harm.

### Why do I still smell after showering?

Body odor forms when bacteria break down fresh sweat, so it can return quickly, especially with stress, heat or synthetic fabrics. Wash armpits and groin with an antibacterial body wash, dry thoroughly, wear cotton or linen and wash clothes regularly. If the smell changes for no clear reason, see a doctor.

### How do I know if I have hyperhidrosis?

Mayo Clinic describes hyperhidrosis as sweating that soaks clothes or drips off the hands without heat or exercise, usually at least once a week and on both sides of the body. See a doctor if sweating disrupts daily life, starts suddenly or comes with unexplained night sweats.
