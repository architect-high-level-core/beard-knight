The art of conversation is the skill of putting another person at ease and of being good company without performing. It is not a lecture, a debate or a sales pitch, and it is not the same as the first handshake or the formal rules of a dinner table. A man who can begin a small exchange, listen properly, choose a subject with tact and leave a circle without awkwardness will be invited back. This guide draws on Debrett's and Emily Post: how to start small talk, how to listen, which topics to raise or avoid, how to manage a group, how to talk at dinner, and what to do with a phone.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Good conversation is a balance of talking and listening, not a performance and not a silence. Debrett's calls this the skill that makes a man remembered for tact and good humour.
> - Small talk is not insincerity. It is the oil in the machine. Safe openings include the occasion, the place, the weather and a light remark about sport; the aim is to give the other person an easy reply.
> - Listen to what is actually being said. Emily Post's advice is to empty the mind of the next point you want to make, keep eye contact, and ask for a clarification if you have not understood.
> - With new acquaintances, keep off argument, sex and, unless you know the room, religion and partisan politics. Wait until you know someone before being braver with topics.
> - In a group, share the floor and draw in the quiet person. If a newcomer joins a private pair, include them.
> - At dinner, talk to both sides and do not hold a private seminar with one neighbour all evening. The phone stays away.
> - Leave a conversation cleanly: a short, genuine line, then actually go. Looking over a man's shoulder for someone more interesting is the ruder option.

## What Is the Art of Conversation?

The art of conversation is a back-and-forth in which both people speak and both people listen. Emily Post's current guidance puts it plainly: it is not a lecture and it is not a competition. The goal is not to say everything you would like to say, and it is not to sit in silence. It is to strike an enjoyable balance. Debrett's adds the social purpose. A good conversationalist puts people at ease and is remembered for tact and good humour. He strikes a balance between talking and listening, picks up threads, and leaves the other person sure that he was interested.

This is a different skill from the formalities of a first introduction, and it is different from table manners. Those subjects have their own rules. Conversation is what happens after the names have been exchanged, when the air has to be filled with something better than a résumé or a monologue. It is also what happens in a group, when the task is not only to be interesting but to make sure no one is stranded at the edge of the talk.

The standard is lower than anxious men think and higher than careless men think. You do not need a stock of prepared anecdotes. You do need attention, a little patience, and the discipline not to turn every subject into a story about yourself. Think before you speak, Emily Post wrote a century ago, and the line still holds: most faults in conversation come from not thinking. The man who talks too easily talks too much. The man who never speaks except when he has something "worth while" to say may wear well with intimates and still add nothing to a party.

## How Do You Start Small Talk Without Sounding Forced?

Small talk is the opening move, not the whole game. Debrett's is practical about it. If you have just been introduced, it is polite to follow up promptly and not leave a silence. If the host has given you a clue — how you both know the house, a shared journey, a common friend — follow that. Otherwise a remark about the occasion, the room, the weather or a recent sporting event is enough. The old royal standby, "Have you come far?", still works in a British or formal setting because it is easy to answer. "Where are you from?" and "What do you do?" are standard in America; Debrett's notes that they were traditionally seen as too direct in Britain, and some older people still feel they are blunt. In a U.S. room they are usual, provided they do not become an interrogation about salary, status or postcode.

Do not be afraid of sounding too dull at the outset. The first job is to oil the wheels. A ready expression and decent eye contact will carry a plain remark. The key is to give the other person an easy opportunity to respond. Open questions help: not "Did you like the speech?" so much as "What did you make of the speech?" Once the talk has started, take turns. In a one-to-one exchange, do not look over the person's shoulder for more amusing company. If you are trapped by a bore, Debrett's says it is more polite to escape quickly than to scan the room while he is still speaking.

Ask questions, but do not interrogate and do not try to "get a fix" on a person by discovering exactly where they live or what they earn. Indirect questions and a little patience are enough. People will usually indicate whether they have children or a partner; there is no need to ask directly in the first five minutes. Common ground arrives if you leave room for it. Forced wit, a controversial opening and a story designed to impress are all ways of making the start harder than it is.

## How Do You Actually Listen in a Conversation?

Listening is the part of conversation most men skip, because they are busy composing the next sentence. Emily Post's advice is to do the opposite. Empty other thoughts and concentrate on what the person is saying. Keep eye contact, nod occasionally, and use a brief "I see" or "Really?" so that it is clear you have not only heard but understood. Once you have the rhythm of the other person's speech, you can add a longer response without seeming to interrupt. If you do not understand something, ask for an explanation. That habit, she notes, comes naturally to a good listener.

Debrett's goes further when the talk is serious. Listening to a friend who is going through a divorce, a bereavement or the loss of a job is a generous act precisely because it requires you to stifle the urge to respond with your own story. Listening is not waiting to say your bit. It is the decision not to compete with the other person's difficulty. Advice, if it is wanted, can come later. A quick "that reminds me of when I..." is usually a theft, not a comfort.

Interrupting is almost never the right move. The exception is something that genuinely cannot wait, and even then you preface it with a brief apology. It is, however, acceptable to follow a conversation with small acknowledgements — a nod, a short sound, a brief phrase — that show you are still in it. What is not acceptable is the vacant "What did you say?" of a man who was not paying attention. People who are hard of hearing may need a sentence repeated. The rest of us often say "What?" from habit. The cure is to listen the first time.

Personal space belongs to listening as well. Emily Post suggests leaving a comfortable space, about half a metre, between you. A serious conversation in a loud, festive room will feel wrong, and a boisterous one in a solemn room will feel worse. If the setting is wrong, it is better to suggest moving or postponing than to shout across a party.

## Which Topics Should a Gentleman Raise or Avoid?

The safe subjects at the beginning are the ones that give the other person a way in: the occasion, the food, the place, a book, a journey, a sport, a shared acquaintance, a recent harmless piece of news. The aim is not to be bland forever. It is to find, without forcing, a thread that can be followed. Debrett's is clear that trying to be controversial on purpose is a form of showing off, and that one-upmanship looks like insecurity rather than distinction. Social talk is not a competition.

With new acquaintances, steer away from argument, from sex, and, unless you know the room well, from partisan politics and religion. Emily Post's older counsel is still sound: do not let an amiable discussion turn into contradiction. A tactful man keeps his prejudices in check and, when he disagrees, says so quietly — "I don't think I agree with you" or "It seems to me..." — rather than "You are wrong." If the opinions are truly opposed, he changes the subject. Never try to catch a person out. If someone is talking about a subject you know better than they do, it is mean to wait until they have finished and then announce that you have written ten books on it.

Money, other people's marriages, illnesses you have not been invited to discuss, and jokes at the expense of a person who is not there will all sour a room. So will a story you have already told the same company. Post warned against repeating yourself, either by telling the same story again or by going back over details that were already enjoyed. Many things are interesting the first time and when they are brief; almost nothing is interesting when it is told twice, at length.

Once you know someone, the field opens. A real conversation can hold books, work (without a sales pitch), family, grief, even politics, if it is entered in good faith and can be left without a rupture. The test is not whether the subject is "serious." The test is whether the other person is still a companion or has become an opponent.

## How Do You Handle a Group, and How Do You Leave a Conversation?

In a group the rule is to share the floor and to make sure everyone is included. If a known storyteller has the room, Debrett's suggests bringing a quieter person in as part of your response: a name, a question, a door held open. A group that becomes a duet, with everyone else as audience, is a failure of manners even if the duet is brilliant. Watch the edges of the circle. The man standing half a step outside it is your next responsibility.

If someone joins you when you are deep in a private exchange, you give that exchange up, however inconvenient it is, and include the newcomer. A private or serious conversation may simply have been in the wrong place. Change the subject or make a link so that the new person is not walking into a wall. The opposite problem is the man who barges into a pair and takes over. He can be welcomed and, if necessary, gently redirected, but he should not be rewarded with a monologue of his own.

Leaving is a skill of its own. Looked-for company over a man's shoulder is ruder than a short, honest exit. A clean leave is a complete sentence: "It's been a pleasure, I must go and say hello to the host," or "I'm going to refresh my drink, I hope we can continue this later." Then you go. Do not linger in the doorway of the conversation after you have announced your departure. Do not invent an elaborate excuse. And do not, having left, stand six feet away and start a louder, more amusing talk that the first man can still hear.

If you are the host, your job is movement. You introduce people with a useful clue, you rescue the stranded, and you do not let two guests who have just met carry the whole evening alone if they would clearly like to meet others. A good host is a traffic policeman with better manners.

## How Should a Gentleman Converse at the Dinner Table?

Dinner conversation is not a lecture to the whole table and it is not a private meeting with the person on your right. The old, still useful rule is to talk to both neighbours over the course of the meal, and, in a small party, to join the general talk when it arises. Begin with something light that the people immediately beside you can answer: the food, the room, a journey, a harmless observation. Then follow the thread they offer. Cross-table shouting is for very informal houses; in a formal one, you keep the volume inside your own stretch of the cloth.

Do not hold forth on a professional specialism unless you have been asked, and even then keep it short. Do not correct the host's story in front of his guests. Do not turn a pleasant disagreement into a trial. If a subject is becoming sharp, it is the gentleman's job, not only the host's, to offer a new one. If you are a guest, you do not start a difficult topic at someone else's table. If you are the host, you have a duty to change the weather when the table is stuck.

Names matter more at dinner than men think. Use them, but do not over-use them. If you have forgotten a name, a quiet, private admission is better than a bluff. Introduce the person on your left to the person on your right if they have not met. A table where two neighbours sit in silence because no one made the link is a small failure of hospitality.

The rest is attention. Look at the person who is speaking. Do not arrange your next remark while they are still in the middle of theirs. Do not tease a guest you do not know well enough to tease. And do not tell a long story that requires a silence from everyone else unless you are sure the table wants it, and even then keep it shorter than you think.

## What Should You Do with a Phone During a Conversation?

A phone on the table is a third person at the conversation, and a rude one. It suggests that the man in front of you is on probation, and that a more important message may arrive at any moment. In a private talk, in a dinner, in a meeting of friends, the phone stays away: in a pocket, in a coat, off the cloth. If you are expecting a genuinely urgent call — a family matter, a professional crisis you have already mentioned — say so once, keep the phone silent and out of sight, and step away when it rings. Checking a screen in your lap is not hidden. It is only slightly better than checking it on the table.

Notifications are a form of interruption you have invited. Silence them. Do not photograph a table, a guest or a plate unless the host has made it clear that this is that sort of evening. Do not fact-check a man in the middle of his story in order to correct him. If a detail matters and you are not sure, it can wait until later. The urge to reach for a device in a lull is usually a failure of nerve, not a need for information. A short pause in a conversation is allowed. Filling it with a screen trains you, and the other person, to treat silence as a problem.

The same rule applies when you meet someone "by chance" and are already on a call. End the call or step away. A man standing in a doorway, talking into a phone and raising a finger to a friend, has already chosen the phone. Choose the friend, or excuse yourself properly and call back.

A good conversation does not require a device, a quotation or a display of knowledge. It requires a man who is, for a few minutes, actually there.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you start a conversation with someone you have just met?

Follow any clue the host has given you, then offer a simple, easy-to-answer remark about the occasion, the place, the weather or a shared sport. The aim is to give the other person a way in, not to be dazzling in the first sentence.

### What is the difference between small talk and real conversation?

Small talk is the opening that makes a real conversation possible. It is not fake; it is courteous. Once common ground appears, the talk can deepen. Trying to be profound in the first minute is usually a form of showing off.

### How can I be a better listener?

Stop composing your next remark while the other person is still speaking. Keep eye contact, acknowledge what you have heard, and ask a genuine follow-up. If the subject is a difficulty, do not answer it with a story of your own unless you are asked.

### Which topics should a gentleman avoid with new acquaintances?

Argument for its own sake, sex, and, unless you know the room, partisan politics and religion. Money, other people's private lives, and jokes at the expense of someone who is not present will also sour the air.

### How do you leave a conversation politely?

Offer a short, complete reason, thank the person, and then actually leave. Looking over their shoulder for someone more interesting is ruder than a clean exit. Do not hover after you have said you are going.

### Is it ever acceptable to have a phone on the table?

Only if you have already explained a genuine urgency, and even then the phone should be silent and preferably off the cloth. In an ordinary dinner or private talk, the phone stays away.

Conversation is a habit, not a gift. The men who seem naturally good at it are usually men who have decided, over and over, to be more interested than they are impressive. Learn a few clean openings, listen longer than feels efficient, leave a circle without a mess, and keep the phone in your pocket. The rest of a gentleman's talk will take care of itself.
